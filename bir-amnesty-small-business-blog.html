The BIR's Olive Branch: A Field Guide to the Micro Taxpayer Abatement Program

Yes, the same bureau that is prosecuting cosmetics companies and shoe brands is also offering ₱5,000 amnesty to small businesses. Both things are true.

Philippine Tax Law Series | RR No. 4-2026 and the General Tax Amnesty Bill | June 2026

If you have been following the BIR's enforcement headlines lately, the ghost receipt prosecutions, the Letters of Authority landing on the desks of content creators, the threatened Online Kandado, you could be forgiven for thinking the bureau has gone full enforcement-only and forgotten what compassion looks like. You would be wrong, but the forgiveness is understandable.

Because quietly, in the same month that the BIR was filing tax evasion cases against 23 corporations, 56 corporate officers, and 17 accountants, it also published Revenue Regulations No. 4-2026, a one-time abatement program for micro taxpayers that lets qualified small businesses settle years of accumulated tax liabilities, penalties, and unresolved assessments for a flat fee of ₱5,000.

Five thousand pesos. Per approved application. The sari-sari store that stopped filing in 2019 because nobody explained what a stop-filer case was. The freelancer who registered with the BIR, got a full-time job, and then forgot they still had an active registration accumulating unfiled returns. The small trader who closed operations in 2021 but never formally deregistered. The program is designed for exactly these people. It is, and this is not a phrase one uses often about BIR issuances, genuinely good news.

What RR No. 4-2026 actually does

The regulation, signed on June 18, 2026, allows qualified micro taxpayers to apply for the abatement of taxes and/or penalties on covered cases. "Abatement" is the operative word here. This is not a payment plan, not a restructuring, not a compromise settlement that requires you to negotiate a percentage of your outstanding liability. It is a clean waiver of the covered amounts in exchange for the flat ₱5,000 fee. The BIR issues you a Certificate of Availment. The case is closed.

The cases that qualify cover three main categories: delinquent accounts, assessments (whether preliminary or final, disputed or undisputed), and open stop-filer cases, including those for businesses that have already ceased operations. Covered liabilities must relate to cases existing as of December 31, 2025. The application window runs until December 31, 2026.

Annual gross sales below ₱3,000,000 (the micro taxpayer threshold under RR No. 8-2024)

Liability Cap
Total basic tax liabilities and/or penalties must not exceed ₱80,000 per taxable year
📅
Coverage Period
Cases existing as of December 31, 2025, prior taxable years only

₱5,000 flat fee per approved application, paid at your Revenue District Office

Certificate of Availment, confirming the covered case is formally closed
The mixed-income earner rule: If you are both employed and running a small business on the side, the gross sales threshold applies only to your business income. Your compensation from an employer-employee relationship is excluded from the computation. This matters for the many Filipinos who freelanced, ran small online shops, or operated sideline businesses while employed, and registered with the BIR, but whose registration has since become a source of unfiled returns and escalating penalties.
Who should be paying attention to this

BIR Commissioner Charlito Martin Mendoza said it plainly at the program's launch: "Many micro taxpayers have already stopped operating but continue to carry unresolved tax obligations or inactive registrations." That sentence describes a very large number of people in the Philippines.

The stop-filer phenomenon is widespread and poorly understood. When a small business registers with the BIR and receives a Certificate of Registration, it acquires an ongoing obligation to file returns, whether or not it earns anything in a given period. Many small business owners, particularly those who closed shop during the pandemic or pivoted to employment, did not formally deregister. Their registrations remained active. Their unfiled returns accumulated. Their penalties compounded. And because the amounts involved were relatively small by BIR standards, the bureau never prioritized them, but the cases never went away either.

The abatement program also extends to businesses that have already ceased operations. This is notable. The BIR has simultaneously, through Revenue Memorandum Circular No. 47-2026, simplified the procedures for closing and cancelling a business registration, with tax clearances now issuable in as fast as three days for qualified cases. The combination is deliberate: settle the old liability cheaply through RR 4-2026, then formally close the registration cleanly through RMC 47-2026. The BIR is, essentially, offering small business owners a structured exit ramp from years of limbo.

How the process works
  • 1
    Confirm your micro taxpayer status. Verify that your annual gross sales for the covered taxable year are below ₱3 million under RR No. 8-2024. For mixed income earners, compute business income only.
  • 2
    Identify your covered cases. These are your delinquent accounts, assessments, and open stop-filer cases as of December 31, 2025. Your RDO can help you identify open cases in your record.
  • 3
    Confirm the ₱80,000 cap. Your basic tax liability and/or penalties must not exceed ₱80,000 per taxable year. Each taxable year with a qualifying case is a separate application.
  • 4
    File your application at your RDO. Applications must be filed personally or through an authorized representative at the RDO with jurisdiction over your registration. Incomplete applications are denied, so prepare your documentation carefully before appearing.
  • 5
    Pay the ₱5,000 abatement fee. Upon approval, you pay the fee to the BIR authorized agent bank or through available online payment channels.
  • 6
    Receive your Certificate of Availment. This document confirms that the covered case is closed. Keep it. It is your proof of resolution.
One thing the program does not do: It does not absolve future compliance obligations. Availing of the abatement for past liabilities does not exempt you from filing and paying taxes going forward. If your registration remains active, you are still required to file. If you intend to stop operating entirely, the formal deregistration process under RMC 47-2026 must still be completed separately. The abatement clears the past. It does not rewrite the future.
The bigger picture: the General Tax Amnesty Bill still pending in Congress

The micro taxpayer abatement program under RR 4-2026 is an administrative measure, it was issued by the BIR without requiring new legislation. But it exists alongside a larger, still-pending legislative proposal that would grant amnesty to a much broader population of taxpayers.

Senate Bill No. 60, filed in July 2025 by Senate Finance Committee Chair Sherwin Gatchalian, seeks a one-time general amnesty on all unpaid internal revenue taxes for taxable year 2024 and prior years. A companion measure, House Bill No. 2653, was filed in the House of Representatives by Rep. Kenneth Gatchalian. The proposed program is considerably more expansive than the BIR's administrative abatement.

RR No. 4-2026 (Now Available)
Micro taxpayers only (gross sales below ₱3M). Liability cap of ₱80,000 per year. ₱5,000 flat fee. Covers delinquent accounts, assessments, and stop-filer cases. Purely administrative, no new law required. Deadline: December 31, 2026.
General Tax Amnesty Bill (Pending)
All taxpayers, regardless of size. No liability cap. Amnesty rate: 2% of total assets or 5% of net worth. Covers all national internal revenue taxes for taxable years 2007 to 2024. Requires legislation. Minimum fees: ₱100,000 for individuals; ₱150,000 to ₱1.3M for corporations depending on capital.

The general amnesty bill comes with a history worth knowing. In 2019, Republic Act No. 11213 was signed, but then-President Rodrigo Duterte vetoed the general amnesty provisions, allowing only the estate tax amnesty to survive, citing insufficient safeguards against tax evasion. The current bills attempt to address that concern directly: they include provisions on limited bank secrecy lifting for validation purposes, automatic exchange of information, and strict penalties for false declarations of net worth or total assets.

"This gives the opportunity for those taxpayers who may have unpaid or outstanding liabilities to move forward amicably." British Chamber of Commerce Philippines Executive Chair, on the proposed General Tax Amnesty

The bills are still at the committee level in both chambers as of this writing. The International Monetary Fund has flagged concerns that a broad amnesty could undermine regular voluntary compliance, essentially rewarding non-compliance and discouraging timely payment going forward. It is a legitimate concern, and one that has featured prominently in every Philippine tax amnesty debate since 2006. Whether the safeguards in the current bills are sufficient to address it is a question Congress will eventually have to answer.

President Marcos has included the general tax amnesty in his legislative priority list for the 20th Congress. The momentum is there. Whether it becomes law before December 31, 2026, and whether its implementing rules are issued quickly enough to matter, remains to be seen.

What small business owners should do right now

The abatement program under RR 4-2026 is time-limited and requires an application, it is not automatic. The December 31, 2026 deadline sounds distant until it isn't. The RDO process requires preparation: you will need to know your open cases, confirm your gross sales figures for the relevant years, and ensure your application is complete before submitting, because incomplete applications are denied outright.

For small business owners who have been avoiding the BIR because the accumulated penalties seemed too large to deal with, the honest advice is: compute the actual exposure first. Many stop-filer cases involve amounts well within the ₱80,000 per-year cap, especially for businesses that were genuinely micro in scale. The ceiling is per taxable year, which means each year is evaluated separately, a business that stopped filing in 2020 and never restarted has five potentially eligible years, each with its own ₱5,000 application. That is ₱25,000 to resolve five years of accumulated anxiety, which is considerably less than the legal fees you would pay to dispute each assessment individually.

The bottom line
The BIR does not usually issue programs this straightforward. RR No. 4-2026 is, by the bureau's own standards, unusually accessible: a clear threshold, a fixed fee, a defined scope, and a real deadline. For the thousands of micro taxpayers carrying old BIR ghosts, unfiled returns, dormant registrations, unresolved assessments they never had the money or energy to fight, this is the closest thing to a clean slate the system has offered in years. It will not last indefinitely. If you qualify, use it. The bureau will not always be this accommodating, and the enforcement side of the house, the one filing cases against Ever Bilena and World Balance, is still very much open for business.
